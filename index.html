about this computer

what this is

a thesis, built as a computer. as digital places optimize for efficiency, prediction and money, the parts that let people meet, say something, and be credited for it get removed, hidden or flattened, because optimization is close to the only thing they are measured against. this is my attempt to show that by taking things away, one profile at a time, and then to try giving them back.

the early web tracked almost nothing and moderated almost nothing. the two came together. later, tracking and ranking both became constant and quiet. nobody voted on the trade.

who made what (images, and what was invented)
what this prototype is testing
QUESTION: as digital environments optimize interaction for efficiency, prediction, and monetization, what gets removed, concealed, or flattened along the way — and can that removal be made visible again, inside the interface itself, rather than argued about outside it?
INTERVENTION: five prior prototypes plus a data receipt are combined into one inhabitable desktop, driven by a shared era timeline: dragging the timeline forward doesn't just restyle the page, it changes which features are visible, how often pop-ups interrupt you, and what's quietly monetized.
TRADEOFF: a synthesis is more atmospheric and easier to feel as a whole, but harder to isolate — it's less clear which specific proposition is doing the work if someone likes or dislikes the experience.
OBSERVATION: read the "what it has noticed" panel in the readme after using a few rooms across a few different eras, and compare the recycle bin to the background click count next to it.
LIMITATION: this is deliberately the least conclusive prototype in the series — it should not be read as the thesis's final answer, only as one way several propositions, and a timeline, might coexist in a single interface.
